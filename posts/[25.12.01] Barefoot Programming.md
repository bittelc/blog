One of the most important talks I've ever seen was about the idea of [Barefoot Programming](https://maggieappleton.com/home-cooked-software); the idea that, with the new superpowers provided to us by LLMs, the software engineering industry may be in its golden age of building useful tools for local communities. Now that we engineers are so much more capable of building software quickly (we are), we are no longer forced to spend all our time building for the companies we work for (we aren't).

And while there is an immense amount to be discussed about the risks and safety challenges that our society has to reckon with now with LLMs beings as good as they are, this small fact is absolutely true- software engineers today have capabilities now that 5 years ago could only be dreamed of. The fact that I haven't written my own code in 7 months is only one example of this.

Barefoot programming asks us to give back to humanity in opposition to LLMs risk taking from it. It asks of us to build for local, small communities that make big differences to few. Can we find problems for our town, for our coffee shop, for our family? Can we devote a fraction of our new superpowers to a project that has no disguise of making money?

These are big, lofty goals and in my 2 years attempting to barefoot program, I admit that I have totally failed. Not because the superpowers aren't as substantial as suggested (they are), but because it is damn hard to find a local problem to solve when compared with the alternative of sitting complacently looking at my work's task inbox and seeing problems drop in squarely and well defined. Talking with people about their very real problems is hard. Sculpting a software-based solution out of those problems is an entire career's skillset, as I've now learned.

Another challenge that I've run up against is that solo-projects can be quite lonely. To be a single engineer attempting to scope, architect and build (even with the help of a "friendly" LLM) is work that requires grit and commitment that will be heavily tested in week 13. When v1 of the program is delivered and then to discover "oh actually there's a whole shitload of things that I didn't think to tell you while you were building us this free program, can you fix these in v2" is a conversation that can feel hopeless and ungrateful. We envision our barefoot program to be received with our local community holding flags and banners with our name on it and a million GitHub stars. Instead, reality is much more lonely and merciless. Building something for free is not sufficient for ensuring the something gets used and solves the problem.

My hope is that the aspirations of barefoot programming are taken up by the software engineering industry. I believe they can be. The staggering amount of ideas that I've seen going into "build" phase is very different than 2 years ago. But the very real, and still very human category challenges will need us to build new skills and new ways to support each other.
